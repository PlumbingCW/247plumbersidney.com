A tankless water heater heats water only when a tap opens, instead of keeping a large tank hot all day. It is a good fit for some homes and a poor one for others, and the difference usually comes down to flow rate, fuel and the piping already in the walls.

This page covers how to size a unit, what a retrofit involves, how tankless compares with a tank, and why flushing matters on our local water.

Sizing

Sizing by flow rate and temperature rise

Tankless units are rated by how many gallons per minute they can heat to a given temperature rise. The rise is the difference between the incoming water temperature and the temperature you want at the tap. A higher rise or a higher flow means a bigger unit.

To size one, we add up the fixtures that may run at once. A shower, a dishwasher and a running kitchen tap use more together than any single fixture alone. We then pick a unit that covers that peak, and we check that the house can supply the gas or electricity it needs.

An undersized unit gives you lukewarm water when two fixtures run. An oversized one costs more up front with no real gain. The sizing conversation is where most tankless decisions are made.

Side by side

Tank or tankless: what actually changes

Neither type is better in every house. This comparison shows the trade-offs plainly.

Storage tank

  • Holds a set volume of hot water and can run out in heavy use.
  • Simple venting and fewer new parts for most homes.
  • Uses standing heat to keep the tank ready all day.
  • Takes more floor space than a wall unit.
  • Usually lasts roughly 8 to 12 years, and a leak often means replacement.

Tankless unit

  • Heats on demand, so hot water does not run out as long as the unit is sized well.
  • Often needs new venting, a larger gas line or a bigger electrical circuit.
  • Has no standing tank to keep hot.
  • Mounts on a wall and frees floor space.
  • Needs regular descaling, especially where water carries minerals.
Fuel choice

Gas or electric tankless

Gas tankless units burn a lot of fuel quickly, so they often need a larger gas line than an older tank did. They also need sealed venting that suits the unit, often a special stainless or plastic pipe, and a condensate drain on high-efficiency models.

Electric tankless units need a heavy electrical circuit, and large whole-house models can need more panel capacity than an older home has. They have no flue, which makes them easier to place, but the panel work can decide the whole project.

We check your gas line size, panel space and vent route before we recommend either one.

Swapping from a tank

Retrofit issues in an older Sidney house

Gas line too small

A tank is happy on a small line. A tankless unit may need a bigger one, and the length of the run matters as well as its diameter.

Venting that does not fit

The old flue is rarely the right vent for a tankless unit. A new path through the wall or roof is often required.

Not enough electrical capacity

A whole-house electric unit can draw more than an older panel can supply. We check before you buy.

Old pipes and valves

Corroded shutoffs and unions are replaced at the same visit. Isolation valves are added so the unit can be flushed later.

Minimum flow needs

Some units need a minimum flow to fire. Low-flow fixtures can end up with cold or inconsistent water, so we discuss your fixtures.

Scale

Tankless flushing and descaling in Sidney

Scale is a hard mineral coating that forms inside the heat exchanger of a tankless unit. Because the water passes through narrow channels at high heat, even a thin layer cuts flow and raises the temperature the unit has to work against. The city's 2022 water quality report listed total hardness of about 113 ppm, in the moderately hard range on the USGS scale.

That number is from one report and may not match your tap today, but it is a reason to plan for scale and not ignore it. Flushing means connecting a small pump and hoses to the service valves, then circulating a descaling solution through the heat exchanger for a set time before rinsing with clean water.

It matters most when you notice fluctuating temperature, lower flow, an error code or a unit that rumbles. Many makers call for a yearly flush, and a lighter schedule may be fine in a home with softened water. A softener or filter can slow scale at the source.

FAQs

Common questions

How do I size a tankless water heater?

Add the flow of the fixtures that may run together, then match the unit's flow at the temperature rise you need. We do this on a visit by counting fixtures and checking the fuel supply.

Do tankless water heaters need to be flushed?

Yes. Scale builds up inside the heat exchanger, and a yearly flush with a descaling solution is a common maker recommendation. Sidney's 2022 hardness figure is one reason to keep to a schedule.

Can I swap my tank for a tankless unit without new gas piping?

Sometimes, but often the answer is no. Tankless units burn gas faster and may need a larger line and different venting. We check the existing line before recommending a unit.

Is a gas or electric tankless better?

It depends on what the house has. Gas units need venting and a larger gas line. Electric units need heavy circuits and panel space. We compare both for your home.

Do I need a permit for a tankless install in Sidney?

Call the Sidney-Shelby County Health Department at 937-498-7249 to ask whether your job needs a permit. Rules differ from minor repair, and the department decides what applies.

Does tankless water heating mean endless hot water?

Not exactly. A well-sized unit keeps up with your peak demand, but if more fixtures run than the unit can handle, the water temperature will drop. Sizing is what prevents that.
